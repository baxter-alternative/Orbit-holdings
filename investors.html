Curated Research

Reading Room

Articles, theses, and market signals relevant to Orbit's strategy · Updated March 2026

Digest — 11 Articles

The consensus across this reading room is clear and consistent: AI is not disrupting the $370B software market — it is attacking the $6T+ U.S. services economy directly by replacing human labor with autonomous agents that sell outcomes, not access. Sequoia, a16z, and General Catalyst have each published frameworks this cycle making the same call: the next wave of trillion-dollar companies will look like services businesses powered by AI operating systems, and the pricing model shifts from per-seat to per-outcome as a result. Sequoia's infrastructure note confirms the race has already moved from model-building to application-layer monetisation, meaning the window to compound proprietary workflow data — the only durable moat — is now.

The competitive signals are loud. Newcomer documented the VC roll-up wave as Silicon Valley's defining strategy of 2025, with GC, Thrive, 8VC, and Lightspeed all quietly acquiring fragmented services businesses and rebuilding unit economics with AI. BVP launched a dedicated $1B Forge fund targeting software-enabled services at exactly Orbit's acquisition size. OpenAI took an equity stake in Thrive Holdings and embedded engineering teams directly inside portfolio companies to build AI from within — not sell it from outside. Shield raised $100M from Thrive for an AI-powered IT services platform. McKinsey's M&A research is unambiguous: acquirers who deploy AI systematically across an entire portfolio generate materially higher value than those who treat it as an isolated experiment.

The Zyg seed round ($58M, ironSource founders, Lightspeed/BVP) provides the cleanest parallel: the same thesis — build an AI operating system for a fragmented, high-volume industry category — validated at seed stage in e-commerce at the same moment Orbit is executing it in recurring field services. The structural tailwinds are converging: AI commoditisation lowers the cost of building the operating stack, a generation of founder-operators are approaching retirement in exactly the fragmented verticals Orbit targets, and institutional capital has confirmed the model. The question is no longer whether this works. It is who builds the widest data moat, fastest.

1Market size: U.S. services economy is 16× larger than software. AI captures labour budgets, not licence fees — a fundamentally larger prize.
2Competitive urgency: Thrive, BVP Forge, GC, 8VC, and Lightspeed are all executing this strategy now. The moat is the data; the race has started.
3Pricing model shift: AI forces the industry from per-seat to outcome-based pricing — portfolio companies inside the Nexus stack are structurally positioned for this transition.
4Portfolio-wide deployment wins: McKinsey's data shows systematic AI integration across a full portfolio generates significantly higher M&A value than piecemeal adoption — Orbit's shared Nexus stack is the mechanism.

Market Context
The AI-native services roll-up category is forming rapidly. Four distinct archetypes have emerged: (1) institutional AI-enabled roll-up platforms backed by top-tier VCs (General Catalyst), (2) AI-first institutional platforms with frontier model partnerships (Thrive Holdings), (3) single-vertical tech-enabled roll-ups with future SaaS aspirations (Splash), and (4) broad SMB holdcos built around operator networks (American Operator). Orbit is the only player combining a payments data flywheel, a live proprietary AI stack, permanent capital, and a recurring field services thesis.

Thesis (August 2025 — Marc Bhargava & Kate Bender)
GC argues AI-enabled roll-ups represent the next wealth-creation wave beyond SaaS — targeting the $6T+ U.S. services sector vs. the $370B software market. "Software only set the table for the future of services."
Model: Combine proprietary AI technology with strategic acquisitions to expand capabilities and margin simultaneously — targeting "Rule of 60" (10–20% growth + 30–40% EBITDA) vs. SaaS "Rule of 40."
GC identifies and assembles founding teams, provides M&A expertise, integration support, and capital strategy. The "GC Famiglia" shares learnings across portfolio companies in parallel execution.
Key Portfolio Companies — Direct Comps
Titan MSP — IT services transformation platform. Acquired RFA (financial services MSP). Direct competitor to Orbit's Apex MSP Group in the managed IT services vertical.
Long Lake — Multi-vertical services platform with 18 acquisitions across residential, business, and infrastructure services. Closest structural comp to Orbit's overall holdco thesis.
Crescendo — Customer service automation achieving 80%+ interaction automation. Acquiring PartnerHero's call centers — applying AI to take over human service delivery at scale.
Eudia — Legal services AI platform ($1.05T market), combining AI with 300+ legal delivery team. Acquired Johnson Hana. Demonstrates GC's cross-industry roll-up ambition.
Dwelly — UK property management, 6 acquired agencies, 25–30% productivity gains. Validates the model in property/infrastructure services adjacent to Orbit's field services verticals.
Strengths
Institutional capital at the largest scale — "billions committed" dwarfs all other competitors.
Titan MSP is a direct, well-funded competitor to Orbit's MSP vertical; Long Lake mirrors Orbit's multi-vertical thesis at 18 acquisitions already.
GC Famiglia creates a shared learning network across portfolio companies — a structural advantage in playbook iteration speed.
Proven M&A assembly capability: GC recruits founding teams combining AI technologists, domain experts, and M&A operators — not dependent on a single founder.
Weaknesses
No payments data moat — GC's model is AI + M&A, not payments + AI + M&A. The transaction data layer is absent.
VC structure means each portfolio company faces an exit horizon — no permanent compounding capital.
Capability-led AI (delivering outcomes) vs. data flywheel AI — GC's companies improve service delivery but don't compound a cross-portfolio dataset the way Orbit's payment flows do.
Breadth across industries (legal, IT, CX, property, multi-vertical) disperses the shared data advantage; Orbit's field services focus keeps data DNA consistent.
Orbit's edge: GC is the most sophisticated institutional validator of this thesis — and the most important competitor to watch. Titan MSP and Long Lake are direct comps. The key differentiator is the payments layer: Orbit owns the transaction flow across every portfolio company, creating a compounding data moat GC's model doesn't build. Permanent capital is the second lever — GC portfolio companies will face liquidity events; Orbit's holdco compounds indefinitely.

Market Research
$5–15K · 4–6 weeks · Go/No-Go decision
▼

Establish market fundamentals before spending on campaigns. Combine secondary data (Census, IBISWorld) with 5–10 partner interviews to define TAM, ICP, competitive whitespace, and entry channels. A Go decision requires 50K+ TAM, 3+ validated pain points, and identified conference or partnership entry points.

Secondary & primary research TAM / SAM / SOM ICP definition 5–10 partner interviews AP: 50K+ TAM · 3 pain points · GO ✓

  • Map 5+ competitors with pricing, strengths, and weaknesses
  • Identify tech stack integrations and community entry points
  • Validate 3+ pain points and buying behavior through interviews
  • List target conferences, associations, and key partnership channels

  • Validate email deliverability before sequencing — target <5% bounce rate
  • Segment into Tier A (high-fit) and Tier B (medium-fit) for differentiated outreach
